**Past two weeks' readings:**  
*Dante, Paradiso, Cantos I–XII*  
*Luther, The Bondage of the Will, The Sovereignty of God*  

This week I attended Higher Things, a youth conference for young Lutherans. Higher Things consists of worship services, plenaries, breakaways, and activities. This week, I thought I would reflect on one of the breakaways I attended: *Pascal's Gambling Gospel.*

## I. Pascal’s Wager and the Poverty of Self-Interest
Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was an accomplished mathematician, scientist, theologian, and Jansenist apologist. His famous argument may be summarized as follows:

>If God exists and you believe, you gain everything.  
>If God exists and you do *not* believe, you lose everything.  
>If God does not exist, very little is ultimately lost by believing.  
>Therefore, it seems rational to believe.

For many people, this is the first philosophical argument for Christianity they ever encounter. At first glance, it appears persuasive. Blaise Pascal was certainly not a fool, and his *Pensées* remain one of the great works of Christian apologetics.

Yet as I listened, I found myself wondering whether the wager ultimately asks the wrong question.

## II. Faith as Insurance
Pascal frames belief almost like an investment decision.

A prudent gambler weighs possible gains against possible losses. If the potential reward is infinite while the cost is finite, reason suggests taking the bet.

The problem is that Christianity is not merely a proposition to affirm because it maximizes expected value.

A husband does not tell his wife, “I love you because it is the rational choice.” Love that arises only from calculation is not love at all.

Likewise, faith motivated solely by fear of hell or desire for heaven misses the object of faith entirely. Christianity is not primarily about escaping punishment; it is about receiving Christ.

## II. Can Someone Choose to Believe?

Can a person simply decide to believe because the odds appear favorable?

Luther's explanation of the Third Article of the Creed in the Small Catechism states:

>I believe that I cannot by my own reason or strength believe in Jesus Christ, my Lord, or come to Him; but the Holy Ghost has called me by the Gospel, enlightened me with His gifts, sanctified and kept me in the true faith…

The Gospel does not ask us to make a prudent wager; it creates the very faith that receives Christ.

Scripture teaches that faith is not manufactured by human reason or decision but is the gift of the Holy Spirit through the Gospel. One cannot wager oneself into saving faith any more than Lazarus could reason himself out of the tomb.

This, however, does not make reason useless. Reason has an important place in defending the faith and exposing contradictions. But reason cannot produce what only God can give

## III. Pascal’s Real Strength
Despite these concerns, I do not think Pascal should be dismissed. His wager does something valuable.

It confronts the comfortable assumption that religious questions are unimportant. If Christianity could possibly be true, then ignoring it is itself a decision with enormous consequences. Many people spend years researching careers, investments, and politics while giving little serious thought to the claims of Christ.

Pascal forces us to recognize that neutrality is itself a kind of wager.

## IV. Where the Wager Must End
The wager may persuade someone to investigate Christianity, but it cannot produce Christianity itself.

Eventually, the question changes from *“What is the safest bet?”* to *“Who is Jesus Christ?”* That question cannot be answered with probability tables.

It is answered in the preaching of the Gospel, where Christ gives Himself to sinners—not because they calculated correctly, but because He is merciful. The Christian does not trust Christ because the odds are favorable. He trusts Christ because Christ has first come to him.

- - -
This is perhaps Pascal's greatest service—not that he proves Christianity by mathematics, but that he reminds the complacent that eternal questions deserve to be taken with eternal seriousness.
