*A paper for Paideia III class.*

Political authority has long been a subject of debate, particularly when rulers claim authority in matters that extend beyond their proper jurisdiction. During the sixteenth century, questions concerning kingship, religious truth, and the responsibilities of rulers became especially important. Martin Luther and Thomas More approach these questions through two very different literary works. In *Martinus Lutherus contra Henricum Regem Angliae*, Luther directly challenges King Henry VIII's theological arguments. In this work, Luther uses forceful polemic to defend his understanding of Christian truth. In *Utopia*, More explores the problems of political authority through fictional dialogue and the description of an imagined commonwealth. Although their literary methods differ, both writers examine the limitations of political power and the dangers of rulers placing their own judgments above higher standards of truth and justice. While Luther attacks royal authority through direct theological polemic, More uses fictional dialogue and an imagined society to question the wisdom and moral foundations of political power.

In 1521, King Henry VIII published *Assertio Septem Sacramentorum*, a defense of the traditional Roman Catholic sacraments against the teachings of Martin Luther. In recognition of Henry’s defense of the Catholic faith, Pope Leo X bestowed upon him the title *Defensor Fidei*, or “Defender of the Faith.” Luther responded in 1522 with *Contra Henricum Regem Angliae*, directly challenging the king’s theological arguments. Their dispute raises a question concerning the relationship between political authority and religious truth: does a king’s royal status grant his theological judgments a special immunity from criticism? Luther’s answer is emphatically negative. For him, the truth of a theological argument cannot be established by the rank of the person who advances it.

Luther’s response to Henry is notable not only for its theological arguments but also for its harsh language. Dr. Martin Luther does not address Henry VIII as one would ordinarily address a king; rather, he openly insults him: “I am speaking to a lying buffoon, hidden under a kingly title, and speaking concerning divine truths, which it is every Christian man's duty to protect from lying abuse” (Luther). This statement reveals the underlying principles of Luther’s attack. Henry’s title and office may command the respect of his subjects, but it does not make his theological arguments automatically valid. By distinguishing between the king’s position and the man himself, Luther suggests that worldly authority cannot determine the truth in divine matters. Indeed, Luther considers the defense of Christian truth a duty shared by every Christian, regardless of social rank. His willingness to address the king in such forceful terms demonstrates that even the highest earthly authorities must answer to a standard beyond themselves.

Luther’s attack on King Henry VIII was not merely out of personal contempt; it was a challenge to the grounds upon which Henry bases his authority in theological matters. Henry had written in defense of the established teachings of the Catholic Church, yet Luther refused to accept the king’s position simply because he occupied the throne. Instead, he answers Henry’s arguments with arguments of his own, treating the king as a man whose claims must be examined rather than a sovereign whose word must be accepted. Luther does not deny that Henry possesses royal authority, but he rejects the notion that such authority grants him the power to determine divine truth. A king may govern his subjects, but his rank alone cannot settle a dispute concerning Christian doctrine. Thus, Luther’s polemic challenges not kingship itself, but the presumption that royal dignity can lend unquestionable authority to a theological argument. In Luther’s view, the truth of Christian doctrine rests upon a standard higher than the judgment of any earthly ruler.

Whereas Martin Luther directly challenges King Henry VIII’s theological arguments, Thomas More approaches political authority through fictional dialogue and the imagined commonwealth of *Utopia*. Rather than addressing a king directly, More introduces a debate over whether a philosopher should enter royal service and attempt to influence political decisions. Through the character of Raphael Hythloday, More presents a sharp criticism of rulers who pursue their own ambitions rather than the common good. Hythloday argues that the conditions of European political life make it difficult for a wise man to serve a king without compromising his principles. The discussion raises a question similar to the one raised in Luther's polemic: what happens when political authority conflicts with a higher standard of truth and justice?

Hythloday expresses his skepticism toward royal service when he declares, “There is no place for philosophy in the councils of kings” (More 36). The pursuit of wisdom often conflicts with the practical ambitions of political rulers. Kings, Hythloday suggests, are frequently more concerned with preserving their power and expanding their territories, than with accepting counsel grounded in reason and justice (More 14). A philosopher who advises such a ruler may find that his arguments are dismissed whenever they conflict with the king’s interests. The issue is not that rulers lack wisdom, but that the political environment itself may prevent wisdom from influencing government. Hythloday’s criticism exposes the danger of a political order in which the desires of those in power take precedence over the judgment of those from whom they seek counsel.

However, More complicates Hythloday’s criticism by presenting an opposing argument through his own character in the dialogue. More argues that a philosopher should not withdraw entirely from political life simply because those institutions are imperfect. He insists that one should not abandon the commonwealth but should instead attempt to improve it through whatever means are available. He writes, “Don’t give up the ship in a storm because you cannot hold back the winds” (More 37). This contrasts with Hythloday’s belief that serving a king may force a philosopher to compromise his principles. More’s character suggests that refusing to participate in political life may leave injustice unchallenged, and Hythloday fears that participation in a corrupt system will of itself prevent a philosopher from speaking the truth. Their disagreement reveals a tension between preserving one’s principles and attempting to reform society. Through this debate, More poses whether wisdom can meaningfully influence political authority when those who govern are unwilling to accept advice that challenges their ambitions.

More develops his criticism of political life further through the imagined commonwealth described in the second book of *Utopia*. By presenting a society with institutions different from those of contemporary Europe, he encourages readers to question whether the customs and policies of their own societies are necessarily just. Setting up the contrast between Utopia and European kingdoms allows More to examine political questions indirectly, without directing his criticism toward a single ruler. At the same time, the fictional nature of Utopia also complicates the argument, since the society Hythloday describes should not automatically be understood as a perfect commonwealth. More exposes the difficulties of establishing a just political order and the danger of allowing established practices or the ambitions of rulers to escape examination. Unlike Luther, who directly disputes Henry VIII’s theological claims, More invites his readers to question the assumptions upon which political authority and its institutions rest.

The differences between Luther's polemic and More's dialogue also reflect the different risks involved in criticizing political authority. Although Luther addresses Henry VIII directly and with extraordinary hostility, he writes from outside Henry's kingdom and is not subject to the king's immediate power. Henry could condemn Luther's arguments, and yet could not simply exercise the same direct control over Luther's person that he could over an English subject or courtier. Luther was by no means safe from political persecution—his security depended in part upon the protection he received from German princes—but he was comparatively beyond Henry's reach. More's dialogue, by contrast, considers the dilemma of a philosopher who might serve a ruler and must weigh the possibility of reform against the risks and compromises of political participation. The difference between these two works lies not only in their literary methods, but also in the contrast between criticizing a king from outside his jurisdiction and attempting to influence political authority from within it. Since *Utopia* was published in 1516, before Luther's dispute with Henry and long before More's own final conflict with the king, this comparison should not be taken to mean that More was predicting his execution. Rather, the later history of More's relationship with Henry underscores the real dangers that could attend a subject's resistance to royal demands.

Martin Luther's *Martinus Lutherus contra Henricum Regem Angliae* and Thomas More's *Utopia* examine the limitations of political authority through different forms of criticism. Luther's polemic insists that royal dignity cannot establish the truth of theological claims, while More's dialogue explores whether wisdom and justice can meaningfully influence political institutions. Their different literary methods reflect distinct problems: Luther challenges a ruler's claims from outside his court, whereas More considers the difficulty of reforming political life through counsel and participation. Together, the works point out that political authority must answer to standards beyond itself, but they also raise a further question: how can those standards be brought to bear upon rulers who refuse to recognize them? Luther emphasizes the duty to defend Christian truth against royal error, while More leaves readers to consider whether withdrawal from imperfect institutions or participation in them offers the better hope of reform.
