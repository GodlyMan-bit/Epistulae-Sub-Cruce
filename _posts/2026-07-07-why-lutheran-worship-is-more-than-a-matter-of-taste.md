**I. Introduction — The Problem of Treating Worship as Preference**

The question of worship is often treated as a matter of taste. One person may prefer one “style of worship” (if such terminology is even appropriate) while another prefers something entirely different. Some argue that the outward form of the service is ultimately unnecessary as long as the Gospel remains present.

Indeed, it is often argued on the basis of the Augsburg Confession that ceremonies are *adiaphora*—that is, things neither commanded nor forbidden by God—and therefore matters of Christian freedom. However, this is only half the truth.

It is true that the Augsburg Confession teaches that ceremonies are not necessary for salvation. In Article XV, the Confession states that “no one may be forced to observe ceremonies which are not commanded by God.” Yet this same article also teaches that “ceremonies and other practices that are profitable for tranquility and good order in the Church (in particular, holy days, festivals, and the like) ought to be observed.” (AC XV.1)

Therefore, the Lutheran understanding of adiaphora is not that ceremonies are meaningless or irrelevant. Rather, Christian freedom exists for the sake of good order, unity, and the edification of the Church.

Too many debates on worship end with statements such as: “I like this style,” “I dislike that style,” “This appeals to young people,” or “This feels more reverent.” Modern discussions of worship often become grounded in personal preference rather than theological truths. Yet the Church cannot be built upon personal taste. This is precisely why the Lutheran Confessions exist: to root the Church’s teaching and practice not in preference, but in the truth of the Gospel.

Although Christians have freedom in matters of adiaphora, Lutheran worship cannot be reduced to personal preference, because the purpose of the Church is to deliver the Gospel and administer the Sacraments. Therefore, worship practices must ultimately be judged by whether they faithfully serve that purpose.

**II. Christian Freedom and the Meaning of Adiaphora**

Adiaphora does not mean meaninglessness. The Augsburg Confession teaches that the Church is: “the congregation of saints, in which the gospel is rightly taught, and the Sacraments are rightly administered.” (AC VII)

Scripture does not mandate the use of a particular hymnal, setting, musical style, or a specific instrument. This is genuine Christian freedom. The Church is not bound by human ceremonies as though they are necessary for salvation. However, freedom is not the same thing as neutrality.

St. Paul writes in his first letter to the Corinthians: “All things are lawful, but not all things are helpful.” (1 Cor. 10:23) Therefore, the question is not merely whether a practice is permitted, but whether it is beneficial to the faith of God’s people and whether it serves the proclamation of Christ.

A practice may be permissible and still be unwise if it distracts from or obscures the purpose of the Church. Anything that draws attention away from the Gospel and the Sacraments should be carefully examined, and if it cannot serve the proclamation of Christ, let it be thrown out.

This principle applies even to practical matters within the Divine Service. A tool intended to simplify worship may unintentionally become a distraction. A screen or projector may be useful, but it also becomes a distraction. A wrong slide, a laughable formatting error, the acolyte dozing off when he should be advancing the slide, and so on can pull the congregation’s attention away from the Word being preached toward the equipment itself. Even something meant to help service can become a hindrance if it interrupts the hearing of Christ.

The issue is not that every modern tool is inherently wrong, nor that every traditional practice is automatically correct. Rather, every practice must be evaluated by the purpose of the Church. The freedom of adiaphora is not freedom to do whatever we desire, but freedom to choose what best serves the Gospel.

**III. The Purpose of Worship: God Serving His People**

Lutheran worship is not about what we do for God, but about what God is doing for us. The Divine Service is not a performance, nor is it primarily a method of attracting people. Instead, God speaks through His Word, forgives sins through the Gospel, and gives Christ’s body and blood in the Sacrament.

In German, the word *Gottesdienst* is formed from *Gottes* (“God’s”) and *Dienst* (“service”). This term expresses the Lutheran understanding of worship: it is God’s service to His people.

Therefore, any practice within the Divine Service must be evaluated by whether it directs attention toward Christ and His saving Word and whether it serves the faithful reception of His gifts.

**IV. The Form of Worship Teaches**

Every practice within the Divine Service communicates something. Worship is not merely a collection of actions performed in a particular order; it is a confession of what the Church believes.

The words of the service teach, but so do the actions. Confession and Absolution communicate that sin is not a minor problem to be ignored, but a serious offense from which we need forgiveness. The making of the sign of the cross reminds us that we have been marked with Christ’s cross in Baptism and that our salvation rests entirely upon His work.

Even the bodily actions of worship make a confession of theology. Kneeling expresses humility and repentance before God, while standing for the reading of the Holy Gospel confesses that the Gospel is not merely information about Christ, but the living proclamation of Christ Himself among His people.

Therefore, the question is not only, “Is this allowed?” but also, “What does this teach?”

Since every form of worship communicates something, the Church must carefully consider whether its practices confess the truth of the Gospel and direct the congregation toward Christ.

**V. The Lutheran Identity: Receiving What Has Been Handed Down**

Lutheran worship did not emerge from nothing. The Reformers did not seek to create an entirely new form of worship, but rather to preserve and reform the Church’s historic worship according to the Gospel. They retained what was faithful from the ancient Christian tradition while rejecting or reforming practices that obscured the Gospel.

The Lutheran Church has inherited a rich liturgical tradition. Across the course of history, countless hymns have been written that confess the great truths of Scripture: the incarnation of Christ, His suffering and death, His resurrection, and the promise of salvation given to His people.

These hymns are not merely songs chosen because they are familiar or aesthetically pleasing. They are confessions placed upon the lips of the congregation. Through them, generations of Christians have confessed the same faith and proclaimed the same Gospel.

The liturgy, the hymns, and the historic practices of the Church are not valuable simply because they are old. Their value comes from the truths they confess. They serve as a means by which the Church continues to hand down the faith once delivered to the saints.

To abandon these gifts simply because they are ancient is to forget that the Church is not merely a gathering of the present generation, but a communion of believers across time who confess the same Lord.

**VI. The Temptation of Becoming Something Else**

A desire to become something else can weaken the identity of the Church. The Lutheran Church has a distinct confession, and its worship should reflect that confession. When worship is shaped primarily by cultural trends or by the practices of other traditions, the Church risks losing the very things that make it Lutheran. The danger is not simply adopting different musical styles or ceremonies; the danger is forgetting why those practices exist in the first place.

The question is not whether the Church may ever change. Throughout history, the Church has adapted to different circumstances while remaining faithful to the Gospel. The issue is not “old equals good” or “new equals bad.” Rather, the question is whether changes serve the proclamation of Christ and the faithful administration of His gifts.

The Lutheran Church does not need to imitate other traditions in order to remain relevant. Its identity does not come from following the changing preferences of the world, but from faithfully confessing the unchanging Gospel of Jesus Christ.

Therefore, the Church should not ask, “How can we make Lutheran worship look more like another tradition?” Instead, it should ask, “How can we most faithfully deliver Christ’s gifts to His people?”

**VII. Conclusion**

The question of worship is ultimately not a question of personal preference. It is not about what “style” is most appealing or what appears most successful. The Church does not gather around the tastes of its members but around the gifts of Christ.

Christian freedom is a great gift, but it is freedom ordered towards faithfulness. In matters of adiaphora, the Church is free to make decisions, but those decisions must serve the purpose of the Church: that the Gospel would be rightly taught and the Sacraments rightly administered.

The question is not merely, “Is this allowed?” but, “Does this faithfully confess Christ?” Does this practice direct the congregation toward repentance and forgiveness? Does it proclaim Christ crucified and risen? Does it faithfully deliver the gifts God has promised to His people?

The Lutheran Church has inherited a rich treasure of worship, hymnody, and liturgical practice—not because these things are valuable due to their age, but because they have long served the proclamation of the Gospel. This reminds us that we are not the first generation of Christians, nor the last. We stand within the communion of Saints, receiving what has been handed down and faithfully passing it on.

Lutheran worship is therefore more than a matter of taste. It is a confession of faith. It is the Church gathered around Christ, receiving His Word, His forgiveness, and His gifts.
